Where was this recorded?
Ask the aircraft overhead.

A propeller aircraft or jet passing a microphone leaves a falling tone in the audio: the Doppler curve. Its shape depends on how fast the aircraft flew and how close it came. Aircraft broadcast their positions (ADS-B), and those tracks are archived. adoppler finds the curves in a recording and searches for the microphone position at which the archived flights reproduce all of them at once.

How it works

  1. Detect. A spectrogram is enhanced for steady tones; tonal lines are tracked, grouped into harmonics and fitted with a straight-line pass. That gives a speed, a closest distance and a time for each event.
  2. Fetch flights. Historical ADS-B positions for the recording window come from the adsb.lol archive (ODbL), adsb.aero, or your own file.
  3. Search. For every candidate microphone position, each event is compared with the curve every nearby flight would have produced, including sound travel time, the air temperature and an unknown clock offset.
  4. Report honestly. The best position comes with an event bootstrap, leave-one-out runs, a time-shift null test and a verdict (ok, weak, ambiguous, failed). The prediction is hash-stamped before any reference is entered, so validation cannot leak into it.

One pass, one curve

An aircraft flies a straight line past the microphone. Change the pass and watch the received tone. The curve is computed from sound emission time, so the steepest drop comes slightly after the closest approach.

Many passes, one position

Three aircraft with known tracks were "recorded" at a hidden spot (dots, with measurement noise). Move the microphone and the clock offset until the predicted curves (lines) fit all three. A single curve can be fitted from many places; three only agree near one.

The full pipeline, in your browser

The demo is a read-only copy of the local interface, showing a 20-minute synthetic recording: five aircraft with ADS-B, one without, three silent ADS-B tracks, a passing car, wind, hum and a ground reflection, a recording clock that is 1.6 s off and a temperature setting 3 °C too low. The microphone position was hidden from the search and compared only afterwards.

Events detected13 (7 pass the quality filter, 5 explained by ADS-B flights)
Error against the hidden position9 m (90% bootstrap radius 25 m)
Clock offset fitted1.78 s ± 0.25 s (true 1.6 s)
Run time on a laptopabout 2 minutes, rendering included

Synthetic tests only show that the method is internally consistent: they use the same physics that generated the audio. Accuracy on real recordings has to come from independent validation. Real-recording validation is in progress; results will be published as error distances, never as locations.

Run it on your own recording

Processing stays on your computer. You need Python 3.10 or newer.

git clone https://github.com/Espaye/aircraft-doppler
cd aircraft-doppler
python -m pip install .
adoppler serve

The browser interface opens at http://127.0.0.1:8765. Create a case from a WAV or FLAC file with its UTC start time and a rough search area, or render the synthetic example first. Command-line use, the case format and instructions for coding agents are in the README.

What it needs and where it fails

  • At least two, better five or more, audible aircraft passes that are in the ADS-B archive. Light aircraft and helicopters without ADS-B, road traffic and music cannot be matched.
  • A recording start time known to within a few seconds; the offset itself is fitted.
  • Unedited audio: time stretching, cuts or pitch changes break the physics.
  • The tool reports "weak", "ambiguous" or "failed" instead of a confident wrong answer when the evidence is thin.

Responsible use

Locating a recording can locate a person. Analyse recordings you made or have permission to analyse, and do not publish positions derived from other people's recordings without their consent.
